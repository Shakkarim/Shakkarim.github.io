Research

I study multiscale severe-weather processes with high-resolution numerical models, radar and satellite observations, and build forecasting systems that turn that science into operational guidance.

Current & recent projects

Texas Ensemble Prediction System (TEPS)

National Wind Institute, Texas Tech University · 2026 – present

TEPS is a 100-member, 2-km convection-allowing WRF ensemble that produces 48-hour severe-weather forecasts for Texas. I develop and maintain the system and lead its move from a fully validated retrospective configuration into real-time operations, including the operational driver, run scheduling, and Slurm/cron job orchestration on HPC.

I also built the TEPS forecast products and deployed the product website: ensemble-mean, probabilistic and neighborhood-probability fields, paintball and plume displays, soundings, and member-level views. The products are presented to NWS forecasters, emergency managers, and partner institutions to show which are ready for operational use.

Multi-Doppler radar wind retrieval for turbulent convection

Severe Weather Research Center, Hampton University · 2024 – 2026

I developed a ground-based multi-Doppler wind retrieval algorithm to resolve turbulent wind structure inside convection. The algorithm is tested within an LES-based radar simulator framework, where retrieved winds can be compared directly against the known model truth, and retrieval errors and uncertainty are quantified.

This work is published in Remote Sensing (2026) and supports observation of severe weather with the Skyler-II phased-array radar.

Large-eddy simulation of convection and boundary-layer turbulence

Severe Weather Research Center, Hampton University · 2024 – 2026

High-resolution LES experiments resolve boundary-layer turbulence and convective eddies, providing the turbulent fields that drive the radar simulator and a testbed for how well turbulence statistics such as TKE can be recovered from radar observations.

Tropical cyclone–urban interaction

Hampton University · 2025 – 2026

How do realistic urban environments affect hurricanes after landfall? This project builds a conceptual and modeling framework to examine how urban surfaces and infrastructure modulate post-landfall tropical cyclone dissipation. I led a collaborative NSF proposal on this topic in 2025.

Urban impacts on convection over Chicago

NC A&T State University · 2022 – 2024

Using observations and multiscale numerical simulations, this two-part study examines how the city of Chicago and Lake Michigan shape the evolution of warm-season convective squall lines and their precipitation patterns (Atmosphere, 2025).

Fire weather and wildfire-conducive environments

NC A&T State University · 2017 – 2024

My doctoral and postdoctoral work investigated the multiscale dynamics behind dangerous fire weather: the orographic processes producing downslope windstorms during the Santa Ana–driven Cedar Fire, and the convective density currents that abruptly redirected winds near the Yarnell Hill Fire, where 19 firefighters were entrapped. I developed a conceptual model describing the stages of Santa Ana wind-driven wildfire evolution, and used WRF with RCP climate scenarios to assess future wildfire-conducive environments with the USDA Forest Service.

Tropical cyclone tracks, waves and orographic rainfall

NC A&T State University · 2017 – 2024

This line of work examines track deflection of Tropical Cyclone Sidr (2007) at landfall over Bangladesh, moist flow-regime transition and heavy orographic rainfall as typhoons cross Taiwan's Central Mountain Range, and the forcing mechanisms of African easterly waves that precede Atlantic hurricane genesis.
